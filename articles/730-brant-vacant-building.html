Unfinished Burlington

730 Brant sat empty for more than a decade. Then it caught fire.

A four-storey redevelopment was approved in 2016. Ten years later, the old building was still there. Who owns it, and why is it still standing?

At 730 Brant Street, the unanswered question is not whether somebody once had a plan. The public record shows a plan in unusual detail. What it does not show is why that plan never became a building.

On Jan. 25, 2016, Burlington's Committee of Adjustment approved almost every requested variance for a proposed redevelopment of 730 and 760 Brant Street. The proposal called for a four-storey condominium with retail along Brant. The committee rejected only a requested reduction in accessible parking.

The long variance list dealt with practical details: vehicle access, setbacks, landscaping, underground parking and fewer parking spaces than the zoning rules normally required. It was the kind of approval that moves a private project closer to construction. It was not an order requiring the owner to build.

The fire exposed the cost of waiting

In February 2026, a fire on the main floor sent 10 trucks and 33 Burlington firefighters to the property. It took about four hours to control. Propane tanks stored inside exploded, no injuries were reported and the damage was estimated at $100,000.

BurlingtonToday reported that the building had been vacant for more than a decade and had previously housed a rehabilitation clinic and beauty salon. A fire department representative said it had been vandalized several times in recent years. The cause remained under investigation in that report.

So why did the City not do something?

The premise needs a correction. A planning approval is permission to develop private land. It is not a construction deadline, and it does not transfer the property to the City when work stalls.

The City can enforce property standards, fire safety and building rules. It can issue orders when a property violates those rules. But a safe, secured private building can remain empty while an owner pursues financing, permits, a sale or a different plan. None of those possible explanations has been confirmed for 730 Brant.

The available 2016 record also does not settle who owns the property today. Registered ownership is established through Ontario's land-title system, not an old planning post or a business directory. Burlington News has not completed a current parcel-register search, so naming an owner here would be guessing.

The record explains what could have been built. It does not explain why nothing was.

Is this Burlington's longest-abandoned building?

There is no public City ranking of abandoned buildings by vacancy date. That makes the social-media version of this story, “the building abandoned the longest,” impossible to verify from the available evidence.

What can be verified is still striking: a prominent Brant Street property was reported vacant for more than a decade, a redevelopment route existed in 2016, and the old structure remained in place when fire crews arrived ten years later.

What comes next: Burlington News is seeking the current parcel register, the status of any active building or demolition permits, property-standards orders and the reason the 2016 project did not proceed. This article will be updated when those records are obtained.
