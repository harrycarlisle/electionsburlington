A bat in Burlington tested positive for rabies. Here's what that actually means.

Halton Public Health confirmed the bat on Aug. 14. The notice did not identify the exact Burlington location, but it did answer the more important question: what should you do if a bat may have touched you, your child or your pet?

By Burlington News · Aug. 25, 2026 · 5-minute read

The headline sounds alarming because rabies is one of the few infections where waiting for symptoms can be fatal.

But the public-health notice does not mean there is a broad rabies outbreak in Burlington. Halton Region confirmed that one bat found in Burlington on Aug. 11 tested positive. It was the first confirmed rabid bat in Halton Region in 2026 at the time of the announcement.

Halton's release did not name the street, neighbourhood or building where this bat was found. That omission matters: Burlington News cannot honestly tell readers whether it was near downtown, Aldershot, Millcroft or anywhere else. Earlier Halton notices have sometimes identified an intersection or neighbourhood, but this one did not.

How does a bat even get tested?

A bat is not tested because somebody sees it flying outside. Testing generally happens after a bat is captured or submitted because there may have been contact with a person or animal, or because the animal is sick, injured or behaving abnormally.

Halton advises residents not to touch or capture bats themselves. If a bat is available after a possible exposure, local animal-control services can help arrange what happens next. A bat may then be sent for laboratory testing so public-health officials can rule rabies in or out.

What the Aug. 14 notice did not say: Halton did not disclose the exact location of the Burlington bat or whether a person was exposed. The release focused on what residents should do after possible contact.

What counts as an exposure?

The obvious cases are a bite or scratch. The less obvious case is direct contact with a bat, because bat teeth are small enough that a bite can sometimes go unnoticed.

Halton says people should seek medical advice after a bite, scratch or possible saliva exposure to broken skin, the eyes, nose or mouth. Its bat guidance also says that if a bat touched your skin, wash the area and contact a health-care provider even if you did not notice a bite or scratch.

If a bat is simply flying outdoors and nobody touched it, that is not the same thing as an exposure.

Why public health moves quickly

Rabies attacks the brain and spinal cord. Once symptoms begin, the infection is almost always fatal. The reason the advice is so urgent is that post-exposure vaccination is highly effective before symptoms appear.

That means the practical rule is simple: if there is a real possibility of direct contact, do not wait to see how you feel. Wash any wound with soap and water, seek medical attention and report an animal bite or scratch to Halton Public Health at 311.

What about pets?

Dogs and cats are protected mainly by keeping their rabies vaccinations current. If your pet had contact with a bat or other wild animal, Halton recommends contacting a veterinarian and public health for advice rather than trying to assess the animal yourself.

For a bat found inside a home, Halton says not to move it yourself. Burlington Animal Shelter and Control is the local animal-control contact.

Is one positive bat unusual?

It is notable, but it is not unprecedented. Halton's rabies history lists confirmed Burlington bats in several previous years, including 2025, 2024 and 2023. The useful lesson is not that Burlington suddenly has a new rabies problem. It is that bats remain a real, if uncommon, rabies source and direct contact deserves immediate attention.

What to do after possible contact: wash bites or scratches thoroughly with soap and water, seek medical care immediately, call Halton Public Health at 311 and do not handle the bat yourself.
