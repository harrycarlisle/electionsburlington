Made in Burlington

Four Burlington technologies that ended up around the world

Water treatment systems, laboratory robots, broadcast hardware and plant-based materials all have deep Burlington roots.

By Burlington NewsAugust 29, 20265 min read
Topics:Made in Burlington

Burlington's biggest exports are not always things a shopper would recognize. Some of the city's most global products sit inside water plants, research labs, television facilities and factories.

Four local technology stories show how far that reach goes.

Water systems built for places that cannot afford to wait

Aslan Technologies designs and builds packaged water and wastewater systems from Burlington. The company has supplied more than 3,000 turnkey, packaged and modular systems around the world, according to Burlington's economic-development agency.

Those systems include drinking-water treatment, sewage treatment and chemical-injection equipment. Aslan has also supplied packaged purified water systems to communities hit by natural disasters.

The interesting part is the format. Instead of waiting for an entire treatment plant to be built from scratch, a modular system can be designed and assembled as a package, then shipped where it is needed.

A robotics company that started in a Burlington home

In 1982, four McMaster engineering students formed CRS Robotic Inc. and began working out of a family home on Lakeshore Road. The original idea was human-scale robotics.

The company eventually became CRS Robotics and was acquired in 2002 by Thermo Electron, now Thermo Fisher Scientific. Burlington is still part of that story. Thermo Fisher's laboratory-automation operation at 5250 Mainway designs, assembles, distributes and services robotic laboratory systems.

Those systems move samples and lab plates between instruments, helping pharmaceutical, biotechnology and research organizations automate repetitive work. Burlington Economic Development says the lab-automation systems are shipped worldwide.

Broadcast equipment from Burlington reaches every continent

Evertz is headquartered in Burlington and operates one of its major manufacturing facilities here. Its hardware and software move television and digital video through broadcast facilities, telecom networks, streaming systems and mobile platforms.

The company says its equipment is used in major broadcast facilities on every continent. That makes Evertz one of the clearest examples of a Burlington company whose products can sit behind media watched far outside Canada.

Plant-based chemistry replacing petroleum-based materials

EcoSynthetix is another Burlington-headquartered company with a product that is mostly invisible to the final customer. It makes bio-based polymers used by manufacturers of particleboard, oriented strand board, paper, paperboard and tissue.

The goal is to replace or reduce conventional fossil-based chemicals such as formaldehyde- and styrene-based materials while keeping the performance manufacturers need. EcoSynthetix says it works with customers around the world and has production capacity in North America and Europe.

That means a Burlington company's technology can end up inside a sheet of paper, a wood panel or a tissue product without the buyer ever seeing the company name.

The common thread

None of these businesses depend on being household names. Their products are infrastructure: the equipment or material that makes another system work.

That is what makes Burlington's technology footprint easy to miss. A city better known locally for the QEW, the lakefront and suburban neighbourhoods is also producing and developing systems that move water, automate research, carry broadcasts and change the chemistry inside everyday materials.
