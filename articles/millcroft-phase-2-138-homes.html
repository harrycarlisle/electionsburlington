The mayor's Millcroft update is about 138 proposed homes. Here's what changed.

The video is new. The underlying fight is years old. Phase 2 would put 73 detached homes and 65 townhouses on remaining golf-course lands, and the City still lists the application as under review.

By Burlington NewsAug. 25, 20265-minute read

If you only saw the mayor's new video, it would be easy to think a brand-new Millcroft development had suddenly appeared.

What actually changed is the public attention. The underlying Phase 2 application is the latest chapter in a much longer dispute over whether remaining parts of the privately owned Millcroft golf-course lands should stay open or be converted to housing.

The proposal: 138 homes across six development areas: 73 single-detached homes and 65 townhouses. The status: still under City review, not approved.

Why the mayor is talking about it now

Mayor Marianne Meed Ward posted a public video on Aug. 24 drawing attention back to the Phase 2 proposal and the City's position on protecting remaining open-space lands.

That video is useful as a first-party political source, but it is not the approval record. Burlington News checked the underlying application status against the City's development page, which still lists the proposal as under review.

What makes Phase 2 different from a normal subdivision story

The land is part of a golf-course community where residents have spent years arguing that the open-space character was part of what they bought into. The landowner's position is that portions can be developed through the planning process.

The City has taken the opposite policy position on the remaining open-space lands and has separately asked the province for stronger protection. That means this is not simply a question of whether Burlington needs housing. It is also a fight over what happens when privately owned recreational/open-space land becomes a development site.

What happens next

Planning staff still have to review the Official Plan amendment and draft plan of subdivision. If the application proceeds through the normal municipal process, residents should expect formal notice, a staff recommendation and a council decision before the City's side of the process is complete.

That does not guarantee the final outcome. In Ontario, disputed planning applications can move beyond council to provincial or tribunal processes.

The useful distinction: the mayor's update is current. The 138-home proposal is not newly approved. Treat political videos as a reason to look at the file, not as a replacement for the file.
