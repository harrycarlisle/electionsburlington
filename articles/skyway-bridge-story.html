The way we were

The ship crash that pushed Ontario to build the Skyway.

One collision in 1952 severed a vital road link, flooded Hamilton with detoured traffic and turned a long-discussed bridge into an urgent project.

By Burlington NewsAugust 23, 20266-minute read

On April 29, 1952, the W.E. Fitzgerald entered the Burlington Canal carrying sand and struck the north span of the old bascule bridge. The span collapsed into the canal. A route that had already become inadequate disappeared in a moment.

The collision did not invent the need for a high-level bridge. Traffic had been growing for decades, and planners had argued for a larger crossing before the Second World War. What the crash changed was the cost of waiting.

Hundreds of thousands of vehicles had to be diverted through Hamilton. A temporary replacement took roughly three and a half months. At busy summer points, traffic through the Beach Strip had been reaching about 2,000 cars an hour on a two-lane route.

A ship did not create the Skyway idea. It made delay impossible to ignore.

A bridge designed around ships

The planned crossing had to carry highway traffic above the canal while leaving enough room for vessels below. The resulting structure was designed with 120 feet of clearance. When it opened in 1958, the original bridge stretched roughly 8,400 feet across 75 spans.

The road was ambitious and contentious. Governments argued over cost sharing. Burlington and Hamilton argued over the name. A Burlington petition supporting the “Burlington Skyway Bridge” gathered 5,000 signatures. The province eventually opened it as the Burlington Bay Skyway Bridge.

The first version was also a toll road. Passenger cars paid 15 cents in cash when toll collection began in November 1958. Heavy trucks faced higher rates, and many avoided the bridge, pushing traffic back onto the Beach Strip. Tolls ended in December 1973.

The second bridge—and a very fast first crash

Traffic eventually doubled. After years of bridge-versus-tunnel debate, construction began on a twin crossing. The new bridge officially opened in October 1985 and was ahead of schedule.

Fourteen minutes after the ceremony, two cars driven by officials were involved in a fender bender. It was a small collision compared with the ship strike that started the story, but an almost too-perfect reminder: transportation history is usually written as engineering. In Burlington, it has repeatedly been accelerated by accidents.
