# Burlington News Editorial Operating System

**Status:** Backbone / canonical internal editorial guide  
**Purpose:** Decide what Burlington News should publish, how each story should be researched and written, and what must be true before publication.  
**Audience:** Human editors, writers, researchers, Cursor/Codex agents, and any future automated editorial tooling.

---

# 1. What Burlington News Is Trying to Do

Burlington News should not behave like a generic local-news rewrite machine.

The product promise is:

> **I open Burlington News and immediately know what is happening in Burlington, what I need to know today, how it affects me, and what is genuinely worth reading.**

The editorial advantage should come from five things:

1. **Burlington relevance**  
   The story matters to people who live in, work in, travel through, vote in, spend money in, or care about Burlington.

2. **Broad appeal**  
   The strongest local stories should also be interesting to someone in Oakville, Hamilton, Toronto, the GTA, Ontario, or even Canada.

3. **Original value**  
   Burlington News should add something: reporting, explanation, comparison, visualization, verification, context, data, local consequence, a map, a useful answer, or a clearer framing.

4. **Reader usefulness**  
   A reader should leave knowing, understanding, deciding, doing, seeing, or feeling something they did not before.

5. **Strong packaging and storytelling**  
   Articles should be treated with the same discipline as strong YouTube concepts: clear promise, immediate delivery, curiosity, progression, payoff, and ruthless removal of anything that does not earn attention.

---

# 2. The Core Editorial Principle

## Local subject, universal question

The best Burlington News stories are not merely local.

They are **local stories built around questions anyone can immediately understand.**

Examples:

Weak:

> City approves development application.

Stronger:

> Burlington could add 8,800 homes beside a possible arena.

Weak:

> Ontario cellphone rules return for the school year.

Stronger:

> Can a teacher actually take a student's phone?

Weak:

> Burlington data centre proposal moves forward.

Stronger:

> Burlington's proposed data centre is not what many people think.

Weak:

> Local ultimate team completes season.

Stronger:

> This Burlington team has lost 24 straight games. Why do they keep coming back?

The subject can be Burlington-specific. The **question, conflict, surprise, or consequence should often be universal.**

---

# 3. The Editorial Funnel

Every story should move through the same system:

**DISCOVERY**  
↓  
**IDEA SCORE**  
↓  
**GO / NO-GO DECISION**  
↓  
**TITLE / PROMISE**  
↓  
**REPORTING + CLAIM LEDGER**  
↓  
**V1: ACCURATE / COMPLETE DRAFT**  
↓  
**STRUCTURE PASS**  
↓  
**V2: READER DRAFT**  
↓  
**V3: ADVERSARIAL EDIT**  
↓  
**FACT CHECK**  
↓  
**PACKAGING**  
↓  
**PUBLISH**  
↓  
**POSTMORTEM / LEARNING**

No step should be skipped merely because the story is short.

# 4. Discovery: Where Article Ideas Come From

Burlington News should discover ideas from a broad range of inputs, including:

- City of Burlington decisions and documents
- Halton Region
- Halton Regional Police
- Ontario government
- GO / Metrolinx
- Ontario 511 / MTO
- school boards
- development applications
- tribunals and court decisions
- local businesses
- event listings
- public datasets
- Reddit and community discussion as discovery only
- social posts as discovery only
- Google Trends / current search interest
- national and GTA news with a real Burlington consequence
- YouTube videos and creators as idea leads, not article copy
- old Burlington history and archives
- questions people repeatedly ask
- misunderstood rules
- unusual infrastructure
- local comparisons
- local records / outliers
- old promises versus what actually happened
- things residents pass every day but do not understand

Slow-news days are not an excuse for weak stories.

If there is no strong breaking news, search for stronger evergreen journalism.

---

# 5. The FNR Principle

Burlington News should deliberately look for the overlap between:

- **Familiarity**
- **Novelty**
- **Relevance**

This is the FNR model.

## Familiarity

Does the reader already understand or care about the category?

Examples:

- school
- Costco
- crime
- GO Transit
- traffic
- rent
- taxes
- AI
- housing
- Tim Hortons
- sports
- weather
- parking
- elections
- restaurants

## Novelty

What is surprising, contradictory, hidden, weird, new, or misunderstood?

Examples:

- a rule people assume is different
- a number that breaks expectations
- a plan that nearly happened
- a category moving in the opposite direction
- a strange local exception
- a hidden cost
- a mismatch between public perception and reality

## Relevance

Why does this matter to Burlington residents now?

A strong story often combines all three:

> familiar topic + surprising fact + local consequence

---

# 6. Idea Score: Should This Story Exist?

Every meaningful article idea should be scored before heavy reporting begins.

Use a 0–5 scale for each category.

| Factor | Question |
|---|---|
| Burlington relevance | Does this materially affect or reveal something about Burlington? |
| Broad appeal | Would a GTA/Ontario reader still find the core premise interesting? |
| FNR / current interest | Does this connect to something people already care about right now? |
| Surprise | Is there a fact, contradiction, or situation most readers probably do not know? |
| Consequence | Does knowing this change what someone thinks, does, pays, avoids, or understands? |
| Curiosity | Does the premise naturally create a question people want answered? |
| Originality | Are we adding something beyond existing coverage or a press release? |
| Evidence strength | Can the important claim actually be verified? |
| Visual potential | Can this be shown with a real image, map, chart, document, or useful graphic? |
| Timeliness | Is there a reason this matters now? |

Maximum: **50**

Suggested interpretation:

- **42–50:** priority story
- **34–41:** strong story
- **27–33:** publish only if the angle improves materially
- **20–26:** usually research lead, not article
- **below 20:** do not publish

A high score does not override accuracy.

A story with weak evidence should not publish merely because it is interesting.

---

# 7. The Hard "Why Burlington News?" Test

Before drafting, answer:

> **What does Burlington News add that the source material does not already give the reader?**

Acceptable answers include:

- we mapped it
- we compared the numbers
- we found the old plan
- we verified the claim independently
- we explain what the decision means locally
- we translated a law into a practical answer
- we found the Burlington-specific exception
- we spoke to someone involved
- we built a timeline
- we calculated something
- we compared Burlington with nearby cities
- we found a contradiction
- we clarified a confusing rule
- we created an original visual
- we found what happens next
- we discovered that the widely assumed story is wrong or incomplete

Unacceptable answer:

> We rewrote the press release more nicely.

If Burlington News does not add value, the story should usually not exist.

---

# 8. Broad Appeal Test

A local story gets stronger when the underlying idea works outside Burlington.

Ask:

> If someone in Toronto saw only the headline, would the premise still make sense and create curiosity?

This does **not** mean removing Burlington from the story.

It means framing the local story around a broader human question.

Examples:

- Can a teacher actually take your phone?
- Why do they keep coming back after losing 24 straight games?
- How bad is crime here, really?
- What does a 20-megawatt data centre actually mean beside homes?
- Why did a popular gathering place close if demand was not the problem?

---

# 9. Timeliness vs Importance

Trending is not the same as important.

Every story should answer two separate questions:

## Why now?

What makes this timely today, this week, this season, or this moment?

## Why does this matter after today?

Will the reader still gain something useful or meaningful once the immediate news cycle passes?

The strongest stories often satisfy both.

If something is merely trending, look for a deeper local consequence.

If something is important but not timely, find a current entry point.

---

# 10. The Title Is the Promise

Burlington News should generally develop the article from the title outward.

The title is not decoration added after the story is written.

The title defines the promise being made to the reader.

Before drafting, write:

1. **Working headline**
2. **One-sentence promise**
3. **What the reader expects to know by the end**

Example:

Headline:

> Can a teacher actually take a student's phone?

Promise:

> Explain what Ontario schools can do, what they cannot do, and what happens if a student refuses.

Expected payoff:

> The reader leaves knowing the practical rule, the exceptions, and the likely consequence.

If the article cannot clearly deliver the promise, the title is wrong or the reporting is incomplete.

---

# 11. Start by Delivering the Promise

Do not begin with vague background.

Avoid openings like:

> As students across Ontario prepare to return to class, questions about mobile-device policies are becoming increasingly common.

Prefer:

> **Can a teacher take a student's phone?**
>
> In Ontario, sometimes. But what happens next depends on the school's policy, what the student was doing, and whether they refuse to hand it over.

The article should begin as close as possible to the conflict, answer, consequence, discovery, or question the reader clicked for.

Do not make the reader earn the article.

---

# 12. Action → Consequence

For explanatory, legal, civic, service, and rules-based stories, favor an action/consequence rhythm.

Example:

> A teacher asks for the phone.
>
> If the student hands it over, school policy determines when it comes back.
>
> Refusing can create a separate discipline issue.
>
> So how long can the school actually keep it?

This creates progression.

Avoid writing that feels like a policy manual:

- rule
- rule
- exception
- rule
- definition
- more rules

The reader should feel a story moving forward.

---

# 13. Every Paragraph Must Earn Its Place

For every paragraph ask:

> **What new thing does this give the reader?**

Valid answers:

- new fact
- new implication
- new evidence
- new example
- human detail
- clarification
- necessary transition
- escalation
- contradiction
- consequence
- answer
- payoff

Weak answer:

> It reinforces something we already said.

If a paragraph only repeats or restates, cut it.

---

# 14. No Back-to-Back Listicles

A list-based article is allowed.

A list-shaped reading experience for the entire article is not ideal.

Avoid:

1. rule
2. rule
3. rule
4. rule
5. rule

with identical structure section after section.

Vary the information shape:

- question
- answer
- example
- consequence
- data point
- local case
- quote
- map
- document
- surprising fact
- next question

The article should feel like it is progressing, not accumulating.

---

# 15. Avoid Back-to-Back Exposition

Two or more consecutive paragraphs that only explain background are a warning sign.

Whenever possible, break exposition with:

- a concrete example
- a number
- a person
- a consequence
- a question
- a contradiction
- a visual
- a local comparison

Information should arrive because the reader needs it at that moment.

---

# 16. V1: The Reporting Draft

V1 has one job:

> **Make the article true, complete, and structurally possible.**

Do not optimize for elegance yet.

V1 should include:

- central question
- working title
- source list
- primary-source evidence
- verified claims
- disputed claims
- exact numbers
- dates
- names
- quotes
- Burlington relevance
- unanswered questions
- contradictions
- visual opportunities
- what happens next
- rough order

V1 can be ugly.

It cannot be careless.

---

# 17. Claim Ledger

Important factual claims should be tracked explicitly.

Recommended statuses:

- `primary_source_verified`
- `secondary_source_verified`
- `multiple_sources_verified`
- `firsthand_attributed`
- `unverified`
- `contradicted`
- `opinion_or_interpretation`

Every important number, date, legal statement, quote, record, safety claim, policy claim, or causal claim should have a source.

Do not silently convert uncertain claims into facts.

---

# 18. Source Hierarchy

Prefer:

1. primary source / official record
2. court / tribunal / legislation / dataset
3. firsthand interview or direct evidence
4. reputable local or national reporting
5. expert source
6. community/social source as discovery only

Reddit, Facebook, X, YouTube, or other social content may generate leads.

They are not enough by themselves for consequential factual claims unless clearly attributed as firsthand material and independently checked where needed.

---

# 19. V2: The Reader Draft

V2 has a different job:

> **Make the article worth reading.**

Questions for V2:

- Does the first paragraph deliver the title promise?
- Can the story start later?
- Is the strongest fact buried?
- Is anything repeated?
- Does any paragraph say the same thing in different words?
- Is there bureaucratic language?
- Are there unnecessary proper nouns?
- Does anything sound like an AI summary?
- Are there back-to-back lists?
- Are there back-to-back exposition blocks?
- Is there a stronger order?
- Does each section create a reason to keep reading?
- Does the article lose the Burlington angle halfway through?
- Is there enough human detail?
- Is every paragraph useful?
- Does the ending pay something off?

V2 is where reporting becomes a Burlington News article.

---

# 20. Natural Language Standard

Burlington News should sound like an intelligent person explaining something clearly.

Avoid:

- "It is important to note"
- "It is worth noting"
- "In today's rapidly changing..."
- "This underscores the importance of"
- "As Burlington continues to grow"
- "Only time will tell"
- "Residents are encouraged to"
- "Here's what you need to know" unless genuinely useful
- "There is more to the story"
- unnecessary throat-clearing
- vague setup sentences
- filler transitions
- inflated adjectives
- repetitive conclusions

Prefer direct, conversational, precise language.

Use shorter words when they preserve the meaning.

Do not sound casual at the expense of accuracy.

---

# 21. Sentence and Paragraph Rhythm

Avoid mechanical uniformity.

Warning signs:

- every paragraph is two sentences
- every section starts the same way
- every sentence has similar length
- repeated "But" openings
- repeated "That means" constructions
- repeated question-answer cadence without variation

Use rhythm intentionally.

Short paragraphs can emphasize.

Longer paragraphs can explain.

A one-line paragraph is acceptable when it genuinely earns emphasis.

---

# 22. Cognitive Load

Reduce unnecessary mental effort.

Before publication, simplify:

- jargon
- acronyms
- government terminology
- legal terms
- planning terminology
- long organization names
- overloaded sentences
- unnecessary dates
- multiple numbers in one sentence

Where needed:

1. explain the plain-language meaning first
2. give the formal term second

Example:

> The city can temporarily stop new development in the area. The formal tool is called an interim control bylaw.

Not:

> An interim control bylaw pursuant to Section 38 of the Planning Act...

unless the legal detail is necessary at that moment.

---

# 23. Human Relevance

Even technical stories should answer:

> What does this mean for an actual person?

Possible human consequences:

- time
- money
- safety
- commute
- property
- taxes
- school
- neighbourhood
- convenience
- access
- noise
- recreation
- business
- voting
- health
- uncertainty

Do not force emotional anecdotes into every article.

But do not let local reporting become detached from lived experience.

---

# 24. Reader Value Test

Every article should provide at least one strong form of value.

## KNOW

I understand something I did not before.

## DO

I know what action to take.

## DECIDE

I can make a better decision.

## SEE

I can visualize something I could not before.

## FEEL

I care about a person, place, or issue more than I did before.

The strongest stories often hit two or more.

A story whose only value is:

> I now know a meeting happened.

is usually weak.

---

# 25. Visual Storytelling

Every article should ask:

> Can the reader understand this faster if we show it?

Preferred visual hierarchy:

1. real reporting photo / relevant real image
2. official or rights-safe source image
3. original map
4. original chart
5. document excerpt / annotated record
6. original diagram
7. Burlington News watercolor editorial illustration when real imagery is unavailable

Do not use fake documentary photography.

Do not use generic AI visuals when a real local image exists.

Use the image at the moment it helps the reader understand the story.

---

# 26. V3: The Adversarial Edit

V3 should behave like a skeptical editor trying to reject the story.

Ask:

- Why would I stop reading here?
- What is the weakest paragraph?
- What sounds AI-written?
- What is repeated?
- What claim is stronger than the evidence?
- Where does the headline overpromise?
- What important question remains unanswered?
- What would a skeptical reader challenge?
- Does the article become a list?
- Does Burlington disappear from the middle?
- Is the best fact too late?
- Is there unnecessary background?
- Is there a sentence that can be removed with no loss?
- Does the ending merely summarize?
- Is anything written because "articles are supposed to have this" rather than because the reader needs it?

V3 should make cuts.

It should not merely compliment the draft.

---

# 27. Accuracy Is the Highest Gate

Interest never outranks correctness.

Before publication verify:

- names
- dates
- addresses
- titles
- numbers
- percentages
- quotes
- legal claims
- policy claims
- election facts
- incident status
- opening hours
- event times
- source links
- image credits
- maps

For changing information, include an updated timestamp where appropriate.

Never invent:

- outcomes
- quotes
- photographers
- sources
- article timestamps
- popularity claims
- records
- breaking status
- "most popular" / "best" claims without evidence

---

# 28. Packaging Pass

The article is not finished when the body is finished.

Every article needs separate packaging for different surfaces.

## Article headline

Can be slightly more complete / explanatory.

## Homepage headline

Should maximize immediate interest and broad readability.

## Deck

One sentence.

Should add a consequence, contradiction, or promise.

Do not repeat the headline.

## Homepage one-line hook

Approximately 8–18 words.

Should add a new reason to click.

## Category

Use the most useful reader-facing category.

## Hero image

Should be strong enough to represent the story on home, archive, social, and related surfaces when appropriate.

## SEO title / description

Clear, factual, durable.

Do not turn durable URLs into clickbait.

---

# 29. Headline Rules

Strong Burlington News headlines usually contain one or more of:

- a question
- a contradiction
- a consequence
- a surprising number
- a familiar object with an unfamiliar truth
- a local situation with broad human appeal

Good:

> How bad is crime in Burlington, really?

Good:

> This Burlington team has lost 24 straight games. Why do they keep coming back?

Good:

> Ontario nearly replaced the Skyway with three tunnels.

Good:

> Burlington's proposed data centre is not what many people think.

Weak:

> Burlington City Council discusses development proposal

Weak:

> Everything you need to know about Burlington crime

Weak:

> Local team continues season despite challenges

The headline must remain supportable by the evidence.

---

# 30. Deck Rules

A good deck adds something the headline does not.

Example:

Headline:

> How bad is crime in Burlington, really?

Deck:

> One category of crime is moving the wrong way.

Example:

Headline:

> This Burlington team has lost 24 straight games. Why do they keep coming back?

Deck:

> After an 0–12 season, they changed the name. Twelve games later, they were still waiting for a win.

Avoid decks that merely restate the headline.

---

# 31. Avoid Generic Intro Paragraphs

The first paragraph should usually do one of four things:

1. answer the title question
2. introduce the central conflict
3. reveal the surprising fact
4. put the reader inside the consequence

Do not automatically begin with:

- date
- location
- government body
- broad history
- generic context

unless that information is itself the hook.

---

# 32. Structure by Reader Need, Not Source Order

Do not copy the order of:

- a press release
- council agenda
- government webpage
- report
- transcript
- court decision

Those documents are organized for institutional purposes.

Burlington News should organize for reader understanding.

Ask:

> What does the reader need first?

Then:

> What question naturally follows?

---

# 33. The Curiosity Chain

A strong article should create a sequence of answered questions.

Example:

> Can a teacher take your phone?

Answer enough to satisfy.

Then create the next natural question:

> How long can they keep it?

Then:

> Can they search it?

Then:

> What happens if you refuse?

The article progresses because each answer creates the next useful question.

Do not manufacture fake suspense.

---

# 34. Escalation

Where appropriate, order sections so the stakes or novelty increase.

Possible escalation:

- normal case
- exception
- surprising consequence
- extreme / rare case
- final practical takeaway

Do not place the most interesting fact in paragraph 14 if it can honestly appear in paragraph 3.

---

# 35. Repetition Audit

Before publish, search for repeated:

- facts
- numbers
- phrases
- conclusions
- explanations
- sentence openings
- transition words
- article thesis

If the same point appears twice, decide which version is better.

Usually keep one.

---

# 36. List Audit

Lists should be used when they reduce cognitive load.

Do not use lists merely because they are easy to generate.

Before keeping a list ask:

> Would this be clearer as prose, a table, a timeline, a map, or a visual?

Avoid multiple consecutive bullet blocks.

---

# 37. Ending Rules

Do not end by repeating the introduction.

Avoid:

> In conclusion...

> Burlington residents should stay informed...

> Only time will tell...

Strong endings include:

- what happens next
- the next decision date
- a final practical answer
- a human payoff
- a revealing final fact
- a return to the opening conflict
- a clear verdict

The ending should feel like payoff, not administrative closure.

---

# 38. Article Types and Their Default Jobs

## Breaking / live

Job: tell the reader what is happening and whether it affects them.

Priority:

accuracy → impact → freshness → action

## Explainer

Job: make a confusing system understandable.

Priority:

question → answer → consequence → example

## Service journalism

Job: help the reader do something.

Priority:

answer → steps → exceptions → source

## Original local feature

Job: reveal something surprising about Burlington.

Priority:

character / conflict → discovery → meaning → payoff

## Development / civic

Job: translate technical plans into human consequences.

Priority:

what → how big → where → status → consequence → next decision

## History

Job: make the past feel surprising and relevant now.

Priority:

unexpected premise → evidence → what almost happened / why → what remains today

## Trend localization

Job: take a broader Canadian / Ontario / GTA trend and explain the Burlington consequence.

Priority:

trend → local rule/data → difference → practical meaning

---

# 39. Breaking vs Newest vs Evergreen

Do not confuse article freshness with breaking status.

## Breaking Now

For active, consequential, time-sensitive information.

Examples:

- major road closure
- active police incident
- major GO disruption
- emergency warning
- important City decision occurring now

## Newest

For newly published reporting.

A new evergreen explainer can be Newest.

It does not automatically belong in Breaking Now.

## Evergreen

Can be published on slow-news days if it is genuinely useful or interesting.

Do not lower the quality bar because the news cycle is quiet.

---

# 40. Slow-Day Backlog

When breaking news is weak, prioritize:

1. major Burlington original reporting
2. useful Burlington evergreen
3. development explainers
4. hidden Burlington / history
5. misunderstood laws or rules
6. local comparisons
7. GTA/Ontario/Canada trend with real Burlington relevance
8. maps / infrastructure explainers
9. data stories
10. community questions worth answering

Never publish commodity filler simply to increase article count.

---

# 41. "Would I Send This to Someone?" Test

Before publish ask:

> If I saw this headline in a group chat, would I send it to someone?

If not, why?

Possible problems:

- no surprise
- too institutional
- no consequence
- too local in a narrow way
- obvious answer
- weak headline
- low-value information

This is not the only test, but it is a useful one.

---

# 42. "Would a Non-Burlington Reader Click?" Test

Ask:

> Would someone in Toronto or Hamilton understand why this is interesting without already knowing Burlington politics or geography?

If no, consider whether the angle can become more universal without losing local specificity.

Do not force every story to pass this test.

Some service journalism is intentionally local.

But original features and homepage hero candidates should score highly here.

---

# 43. Hero Story Standard

A homepage hero should usually have:

- strong broad appeal
- a clean visual
- immediate curiosity
- Burlington significance
- original value
- enough consequence to justify prominence

A story is not hero-worthy merely because it is the newest.

---

# 44. The "No Assumed Knowledge" Rule

Do not require the reader to already know:

- a councillor
- a developer
- a ward
- an acronym
- a committee
- a planning process
- a local league
- a previous article

Lead with the understandable idea first.

Introduce names and institutions when they become necessary.

---

# 45. Proper Noun Audit

Too many names increase cognitive load.

Before publish, identify every proper noun in the opening 20% of the article.

Ask:

> Does the reader need this name now?

If not, delay it.

Example:

Prefer:

> Burlington is considering a major redevelopment near the GO station.

Then explain who proposed it.

Do not begin with three corporate entities and a planning file number.

---

# 46. Number Audit

Numbers are valuable when they change understanding.

Do not overload the reader with figures.

For every number ask:

> What does this number mean?

Whenever possible, translate scale.

Example:

Instead of only:

> 20 megawatts

add the relevant comparison or consequence if supportable.

Never use a comparison that cannot be sourced.

---

# 47. Quote Standard

A quote should add:

- voice
- emotion
- authority
- contradiction
- firsthand knowledge
- a unique phrase

Do not use quotes merely to restate facts already explained better in narration.

Paraphrase routine information.

Quote the part only that is distinctive.

---

# 48. Originality Audit

Before publish, compare the Burlington News story against the most obvious competing coverage.

Ask:

- What is our unique angle?
- What did we independently verify?
- What did we explain more clearly?
- What did we visualize?
- What local consequence did others miss?
- What primary source did we inspect?

If the answers are weak, improve the story before publishing.

---

# 49. AI Use Standard

AI may assist with:

- discovery
- source organization
- transcription analysis
- claim extraction
- draft structure
- simplification
- repetition detection
- headline variants
- copyediting
- QA checklists

AI must not be treated as a source of truth.

Any factual claim generated by AI must be verified against reliable evidence.

AI should not invent:

- facts
- quotes
- citations
- source details
- image credits
- outcomes
- current statuses

The final article should read like Burlington News, not like an AI summary.

---

# 50. V1 → V2 → V3 Summary

## V1: TRUE

Question:

> Is this complete and accurate?

Focus:

- reporting
- sources
- facts
- claims
- unknowns

## V2: GOOD

Question:

> Is this worth reading?

Focus:

- hook
- order
- clarity
- natural language
- progression
- value

## V3: HARD TO KILL

Question:

> What is still wrong with this?

Focus:

- overclaims
- repetition
- boring sections
- AI language
- weak ending
- missing questions
- unsupported conclusions

---

# 51. Final Pre-Publish Checklist

## IDEA

- [ ] Strong Burlington relevance
- [ ] Clear reader value
- [ ] FNR present where appropriate
- [ ] Broad appeal considered
- [ ] Original Burlington News contribution
- [ ] Evidence strong enough

## TITLE

- [ ] Clear promise
- [ ] Interesting without being misleading
- [ ] Understandable without assumed local knowledge
- [ ] Article delivers what title promises

## OPENING

- [ ] Starts close to the answer/conflict
- [ ] No fluffy generic intro
- [ ] Strongest relevant fact appears early
- [ ] Reader knows why to continue

## STRUCTURE

- [ ] Sections progress naturally
- [ ] Action → consequence where useful
- [ ] No back-to-back listicles
- [ ] No long back-to-back exposition
- [ ] No buried payoff
- [ ] Local relevance remains throughout

## WRITING

- [ ] Natural language
- [ ] Simple words where possible
- [ ] No bureaucratic filler
- [ ] No AI-sounding transitions
- [ ] Paragraphs vary naturally
- [ ] Every paragraph adds something new
- [ ] Repetition removed

# 54. V2 Editorial Rewrite Prompt

Internal agents may use this as a rewrite checklist:

```text
Take the verified V1 reporting draft and turn it into the reader-facing V2.

Do not change factual meaning.
Do not introduce unsupported claims.
Do not invent quotes, sources, numbers, or conclusions.

Optimize for:
- immediate delivery on the headline promise
- natural language
- simplicity
- progression
- action → consequence where useful
- curiosity chain
- broad readability
- Burlington relevance
- useful examples
- varied paragraph rhythm

Remove:
- repetition
- generic intros
- bureaucratic phrasing
- unnecessary proper nouns
- back-to-back listicles
- back-to-back exposition
- AI-style transitions
- paragraphs that add no new value

Move stronger facts earlier when doing so remains accurate.
Do not merely shorten; improve the reading experience.
```

# 55. V3 Adversarial Prompt

```text
Act as a skeptical Burlington News editor trying to reject this article.

Do not praise it first.

Identify:
- the weakest paragraph
- any repeated idea
- any sentence that sounds AI-written
- any unsupported or overstated claim
- any place the headline overpromises
- any unanswered reader question
- any section that becomes a list
- any section with too much exposition
- any unnecessary proper nouns or jargon
- any buried stronger fact
- any point where Burlington relevance disappears
- any paragraph that can be deleted without loss
- any weak or repetitive ending

Then provide exact recommended cuts, moves, or rewrites.
Do not alter verified factual meaning.
```

# 57. Post-Publish Learning

Publishing is not the end of the process.

Track where possible:

- homepage CTR
- search traffic
- social sharing
- scroll depth
- completion rate
- time on article
- exits
- related-story clicks
- return visits
- saves/bookmarks
- audio starts/completes where applicable

Then ask:

- Did the title outperform or underperform?
- Did broad-appeal stories travel beyond Burlington?
- Which topics created return visits?
- Where did readers leave?
- Which article structures held attention?
- Which story categories were overproduced?
- Which story ideas looked good internally but failed with readers?

Use this to improve future idea scoring.

# 58. What Burlington News Should Become Known For

Burlington News should become unusually good at five editorial jobs:

## 1. Getting around

GO, QEW, Skyway, road closures, parking, commute tools.

## 2. What is changing

Development, taxes, growth, major decisions, businesses, schools.

## 3. What should I do?

Events, food, weekend plans, date-night ideas, open-now utility.

## 4. What does this mean for me?

Elections, laws, school rules, city rules, snow, garbage, beaches, transit.

## 5. Hidden Burlington

History, forgotten plans, infrastructure, odd local facts, buried documents, surprising comparisons.

The best articles often combine more than one.

# 59. Non-Negotiables

1. **Accuracy first.**
2. **Do not publish merely to fill space.**
3. **Every article must add real value.**
4. **Start close to the promise.**
5. **No fluffy generic intros.**
6. **No back-to-back listicles unless there is a compelling reason.**
7. **No unnecessary repetition.**
8. **Use natural language.**
9. **Avoid assumed local knowledge in packaging.**
10. **Prefer familiar topic + novel fact + Burlington relevance.**
11. **Use strong local visuals when available.**
12. **Do not fake timeliness, breaking status, popularity, or certainty.**
13. **V1 verifies. V2 engages. V3 attacks.**
14. **The title is a promise. Deliver it immediately.**
15. **A slow news day is an opportunity for better evergreen journalism, not lower standards.**
