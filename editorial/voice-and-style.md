# Burlington News voice and style

This is a reporting standard, not a request to imitate another publication or writer. Burlington News uses original language, clear sourcing and restrained narrative technique.

## The voice

- Write like a knowledgeable Burlington neighbour who did the reporting.
- Prefer plain, specific language to institutional language.
- Use contractions when a person would naturally use them: “didn’t,” “wasn’t,” “you’re.” Avoid contractions only where ambiguity or solemnity makes the full form better.
- Let facts carry surprise. Do not tell readers that something is fascinating, iconic, shocking or important.
- Vary sentence length. A short sentence should land a fact, not manufacture drama.
- Keep the writer out of the story unless first-person reporting is necessary and disclosed.

## What every feature needs

1. A narrative question that a reader can repeat in one sentence.
2. An opening built from an observed or documented moment.
3. Clean chronology. If the story leaves chronological order, signal why.
4. Stakes specific to Burlington—not a generic regional trend with the city pasted on.
5. A reveal or answer that changes how the opening is understood.
6. Precise attribution at the moment a reader needs it.

## Natural-language rules

- Write “The City bought 84 properties” instead of “Property acquisition was undertaken.”
- Write “Council still had to vote” instead of “The proposal remained subject to the approvals process.”
- Delete throat-clearing: “It is important to note,” “When it comes to,” “In a sense,” “This speaks to,” and “The deeper challenge.”
- Delete self-conscious signposting: “Why it matters,” “The key takeaway,” and “This raises an important question.” Put the meaning in the sentence itself.
- Do not end every section with a miniature conclusion. Trust the transition.
- Avoid the repeated five-part AI cadence: claim, explanation, example, lesson, summary. Paragraphs should follow the reporting, not a template.

## Headlines and decks

The headline makes one clear promise. The deck supplies concrete texture or a second open loop.

Strong deck:

> A ship strike, failed tolls, 84 purchased properties—and a crash 14 minutes after the second span opened.

Why it works: the numbers are verifiable, the events contrast with each other, and the reader wants to know how they fit one bridge story.

Weak deck:

> An opening-day crash shaped the crossing Burlington knows.

Why it fails: “shaped” is abstract, “the crossing Burlington knows” is generic, and the line hides the two strongest facts—the 84 properties and 14-minute timing.

Decks should generally be one sentence, or two short sentences at most. They establish what the story is and why it is interesting. They should not summarize every conclusion or read like a report abstract.

Never make the deck repeat the headline in different words.

## Article endings

Every article must end with a concrete payoff. The final paragraph is the last beat of a good video: answer the main question, then leave the reader with the most useful or interesting consequence.

Never end with generic phrases such as “only time will tell,” “the story is what happens next,” “it remains to be seen,” “that could change,” or “the interesting question is what comes next.”

Choose one ending type that fits the reporting:

**A. The unanswered question.** Use when the reporting reveals a specific unresolved issue.

> The number that still needs explaining is fraud: most property crime fell, while reported fraud kept climbing.

**B. The next measurable thing.** Use when a future decision, data release or event will answer the story.

> The next signal will be the city’s fall traffic count, which will show whether the lane changes actually reduced delays.

**C. The practical consequence.** Use when the story already has a clear reader takeaway.

> For Burlington residents, that means the bigger crime risk may increasingly arrive through a phone or inbox rather than a stolen car.

**D. The new conflict.** Use when answering the main question creates another interesting conflict.

> The city says the proposed facility is not an AI hyperscale data centre. The next question is whether its actual power, water and noise demands support that distinction.

Do not force the same formula onto every article. The ending must be specific to that story.

### Article ending check

Before publishing, ask:

1. Has the article answered its original question?
2. What is the most interesting fact or consequence created by that answer?
3. Is there one specific unresolved question?
4. Is there a concrete next event or data point?
5. What should a Burlington reader understand differently now?

The final paragraph must answer at least one of questions 2–5. Reject generic endings.

## Article length

- A daily service item may be 300–700 words.
- An explanatory story may be 700–1,200 words.
- A weekly narrative feature should normally be 1,200–2,000 words, but only when reporting supports that length.
- Stop when the narrative question is answered. Length is not a quality signal.

## Prohibited shortcuts

- No invented dialogue, interior thoughts, motives, composite people or reconstructed scenes presented as observed.
- No quotation assembled from multiple remarks.
- No claim sourced only to Reddit, Facebook, X or local folklore.
- No fake reporter identities. Use a real disclosed author or “Burlington News Staff.”
- No unsupported “best,” “oldest,” “first,” “beloved,” “everyone” or “Burlington institution.”
- No generated image presented as a photograph or historical record.

## Numbers in prose

Do not make readers parse a paragraph of sequential comparators:

> Halton was 31.2. Durham was 47.1. York was 53.9.

Use prose for the interesting comparison, and a visual for the full dataset:

> Halton’s CSI was 31.2, compared with 66.2 in Hamilton and 68.7 in Toronto.

The same rule applies to year-over-year category changes.

## Visual standard

- Plan at least one meaningful visual every 500–700 words where the reporting allows it.
- Prefer licensed archival photographs, original reporting photography, maps, documents and diagrams.
- An illustration must be anchored to source material, labelled “editorial illustration” or “reconstruction,” and kept separate from evidence.
- Never recreate a copyrighted photograph to evade its licence.
- A caption states what is known, what is interpreted, the creator, and the licence or permission basis.
