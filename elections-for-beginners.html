Elections for beginners

What are you actually voting for?

A municipal election chooses the people who make local government decisions. In Burlington, that means a mayor, one councillor for your ward and the school-board trustee race you are eligible to vote in.

First: what is an election?

It is a vote to choose who gets a public job. Municipal elections happen every four years in Ontario. The winners serve until the next regular municipal election.

Municipal just means local.

This election is about Burlington and the local school boards. It is different from a provincial election, where you choose an MPP, and a federal election, where you choose an MP.

The three choices on a Burlington ballot

Who does what
1

Mayor

Everyone in Burlington votes in the same mayoral race. The mayor leads council, chairs council meetings, represents Burlington publicly and has additional provincial “strong mayor” powers, including proposing the City budget. The mayor is still one member of the seven-person council for ordinary council votes.

2

Your ward councillor

Burlington has six wards. You vote for one councillor in the ward where you live. That councillor represents local issues and also votes on citywide decisions such as budgets, roads, transit, parks and development.

3

School-board trustee

Trustees sit on a school board, not City Council. They bring community concerns to the board and make board decisions together. Which trustee race appears on your ballot depends on your school-board support and eligibility.

One important Burlington detail

Two levels at once

The mayor and Burlington councillors also sit on Halton Regional Council.

That matters because some services you use are not run by the City of Burlington. Halton Region handles things such as drinking water, wastewater, waste management, paramedics, public health, housing services and regional roads. In 2026, the Halton Regional Chair is not a separate Burlington ballot choice under the province's new regional-governance rules.

How a campaign promise becomes a real city decision

It usually takes more than one person
1. Someone proposes itA mayor, councillor, staff member or public process puts an idea on the table.
2. The details get worked outStaff may study cost, law, timing, traffic, planning or service impacts.
3. Council decidesFor normal council business, elected members debate and vote. A candidate cannot promise the votes of everyone else.
4. Money has to existA project may need budget approval, outside funding or a later capital plan.
5. Then it gets built or delivered“Approved” is not the same thing as “finished.” That is why this site separates proposed, approved, funded, started and completed.

Four questions to ask when a candidate promises something

Fast reality check
1

Is this actually a City power?

If another government controls it, what can Burlington realistically change?

2

What would it cost?

If the promise saves money, what gets reduced? If it adds a service, how is it paid for?

3

Who else has to agree?

Most council decisions need enough votes. Some projects also need Halton, provincial or other approvals.

4

How would we know it happened?

A specific target, date or measurable result is easier to check than “improve traffic” or “make life affordable.”
