730 Brant sat empty for more than a decade. Then it caught fire.

Burlington approved a four-storey redevelopment in 2016. A decade later the vacant building was still there when it caught fire.

The obvious question after a vacant building catches fire is what started it.

At 730 Brant Street, there is a second question that may be more interesting: why was the building still there at all?

On Jan. 25, 2016, Burlington's Committee of Adjustment approved most of the zoning relief needed for a proposed four-storey condominium with ground-floor retail at 730 and 760 Brant. In February 2026, fire crews entered the still-vacant commercial building after flames and propane-tank explosions brought 10 trucks and 33 firefighters to the property.

The beginning and end of that decade are documented. The middle is not.

The 2016 approval did not mean construction was guaranteed

The committee approved changes involving vehicle access, setbacks, landscaping, underground parking and the total parking supply. It refused only the request to reduce accessible parking.

That made the proposal more feasible. It did not force construction to begin, create a deadline, guarantee financing or require the owner to demolish the existing building.

This is the distinction that gets lost when a project is described simply as “approved.” Planning permission answers what may be built. It does not tell us whether a developer can finance it, sell it, redesign it or walk away.

Then the cost of waiting became visible

In February 2026, a fire on the main floor sent 10 trucks and 33 Burlington firefighters to the property. It took about four hours to control. Propane tanks stored inside exploded, no injuries were reported and the damage was estimated at $100,000.

BurlingtonToday reported that the building had been vacant for more than a decade and had previously housed a rehabilitation clinic and beauty salon. A fire department representative said the property had been vandalized several times and suspected an intruder or unhoused person may have started the fire.

That was not a final cause finding. Burlington's fire-prevention unit was still investigating.

What we cannot responsibly say: the public records reviewed do not support claims that the fire was connected to insurance, debt or an attempt to escape property costs. Those claims would require actual fire-investigation, ownership and insurance evidence.

The biggest gap is still ownership and project history

Who currently owns the property? Did the 2016 applicant ever seek a building permit? Was the project redesigned, sold or abandoned? Did financing fail?

Those are straightforward questions, but the easily available public record does not answer them.

Registered ownership is established through Ontario's land-title system, not an old planning post or a business directory. Burlington News has not completed a current parcel-register search, so naming a current owner here would be guessing.

The record shows what Burlington allowed in 2016. It does not show who stopped, when they stopped or why.

Is it Burlington's longest-abandoned building?

That sounds like a great headline. It is not something the available evidence can prove.

The City does not publish a ranking of vacant buildings by the date they became vacant. So Burlington News cannot honestly call 730 Brant the city's longest-abandoned property.

What can be verified is already unusual: a prominent Brant Street property was reported vacant for more than a decade, a redevelopment route existed in 2016, and the old structure remained in place when fire crews arrived ten years later. The unanswered question is still the empty middle: who owned it, and why the approved project never replaced the building.

What would complete this story: the current parcel register, active building or demolition permits, any property-standards orders and records explaining what happened to the 2016 project. This article should be updated when those records are obtained.
