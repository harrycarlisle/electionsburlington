A bat in Burlington tested positive for rabies. Here's what that actually means.

The notice does not name a street. It does say when a bat encounter becomes a medical problem.

By Burlington NewsAug. 25, 20265-minute read

The first question most Burlington residents will ask is obvious: where was the bat found?

Halton Public Health did not say.

The Region confirmed that a bat found in Burlington on Aug. 11 tested positive for rabies, with the result announced Aug. 14. It was Halton's first confirmed rabid bat of 2026 at the time of the notice. The release did not identify a street, neighbourhood or building, and it did not say whether a person had been exposed.

That means there is no factual basis for placing this case in downtown Burlington, Aldershot, Millcroft or anywhere else.

What is confirmed: one Burlington bat tested positive. What is not public: the exact location and whether a person was exposed.

How does a bat end up being tested?

A bat is not tested simply because someone sees it flying outside.

Testing usually begins after a bat is captured or submitted because there may have been contact with a person or animal, or because the animal is sick, injured or behaving abnormally. Halton advises residents not to catch or handle a bat themselves.

If a bat is available after a possible exposure, animal-control and public-health officials can help determine what happens next. Laboratory testing can then confirm whether the animal carried rabies.

The harder question: what counts as contact?

A clear bite or scratch is an exposure. The less obvious problem is that bat teeth are small enough that a bite can sometimes go unnoticed.

Halton says people should seek medical advice after a bite, scratch or possible saliva exposure to broken skin, the eyes, nose or mouth. Its bat guidance also says that if a bat touched your skin, wash the area and contact a health-care provider even if you did not notice a bite or scratch.

A bat flying outside with no contact is different. Seeing a bat is not, by itself, a rabies exposure.

Why doctors do not wait for symptoms

Rabies attacks the brain and spinal cord. Once symptoms begin, the infection is almost always fatal.

The good news is that post-exposure treatment is highly effective when it is given before symptoms appear. That is why the decision is based on the possibility of meaningful contact, not on whether someone feels sick afterward.

If contact may have happened: wash bites or scratches thoroughly with soap and water, seek medical care promptly and contact Halton Public Health through 311. Do not handle the bat yourself.

What if a pet touched the bat?

Dogs and cats are protected mainly by keeping rabies vaccinations current. If a pet had contact with a bat or other wild animal, Halton recommends contacting a veterinarian and public health rather than trying to judge the risk at home.

If a bat is found inside a home, Halton's guidance is to avoid handling it and contact local animal control for help.

Does one positive bat mean Burlington has an outbreak?

No. A single confirmed animal does not establish a broad outbreak.

It is also not unprecedented. Halton's rabies history lists confirmed Burlington bats in previous years, including 2025, 2024 and 2023.

The useful takeaway is narrower: bats remain a real but uncommon rabies source, and direct contact deserves immediate attention even when the animal looked normal.
