The stories behind five of Burlington's strangest street names

Snake Road follows an old route up the escarpment. Ghent Avenue came out of a family farm. An entire cluster of streets was named after fruit.

By Burlington NewsAugust 29, 20264 min read
Topics:Local history

Burlington's street map still carries pieces of the farms, families and routes that existed before much of the modern city was built.

Snake Road

The name is about the road itself. Snake Road winds up the Niagara Escarpment because a straighter climb would have been too steep. Flamborough Archives says the route follows a trail used by First Nations people long before European settlement.

In 1853, James Kent Griffin financed a gravel toll road along the route to connect the bay with Waterdown and communities farther north. The road's bends became its name. Burlington later considered straightening some of those curves in the 1980s, but the plan was dropped after local opposition.

The road is unusual enough to deserve its own story. Here is why Snake Road still looks the way it does.

Ghent Avenue

Ghent Avenue sits on land tied to one of Burlington's early settler families. Thomas Ghent purchased land from Joseph Brant in 1804, and the Ghent family farmed in Burlington for generations.

William Ghent bought the Maple Lodge farm in 1896. When he retired in 1909, he divided the farm into building lots, including the land that became Ghent Avenue.

Blairholm Avenue

Blairholm Avenue preserves another family name. George Blair bought a 50-acre fruit farm north of the old village in 1898. The Blair family remained connected to the property for decades.

When the farm was subdivided for housing, the street was named Blairholm Avenue in 1952 to commemorate the family.

Apple Valley, Applevale, Melba, McIntosh, Bartlett and Flemish

This cluster is a map of Burlington's orchard past. The Burlington Historical Society says streets created on the former Strathcona Orchards lands were named after fruit varieties.

Apple Valley, Applevale, Melba and McIntosh refer to apples. Bartlett and Flemish refer to pears. The names survive even though the orchards that inspired them do not.

King Road

King Road was named for one of the area's early settler families. The exact origin of the road's alignment is less certain.

The Burlington Historical Society says it may have marked the western edge of Joseph Brant's block of land, or it may have begun as a laneway separating the properties of early settlers Charles King and George Chisholm. The route also ran along low, swampy land that drained toward Burlington Bay.

That uncertainty is part of what makes the old road names useful. They are not a perfect history of Burlington, but they often point directly at the people, crops and geography that shaped the city before subdivisions filled in the map.
