Why do cars appear to roll uphill on Burlington's King Road?

For at least 70 years, one short stretch of King Road has made drivers question which way is down. The explanations have ranged from magnetism to ghosts.

By Burlington NewsAugust 30, 20264 min read
Topics:Local history/Mysteries

Mark Visser heard about Burlington's magnetic hill not long after he moved to the city. Finding it was harder than hearing about it.

He told BurlingtonToday in 2025 that he spent weeks looking for the right stretch of King Road. The strange part was simple: from the driver's seat, the road looked as if it climbed. A vehicle could move in the direction that appeared to be uphill without the engine pushing it there.

The spot is on a roughly 200-metre stretch of King Road north of Highway 403, past Bayview Park. The road is narrow, wooded and missing one thing that would make the scene much easier for the brain to read: a clean, reliable horizon.

That has been enough to keep the story alive for generations.

The mystery predates the internet by decades

In 2016, the Burlington Historical Society republished an item written by former society member Nora Gilbert in the 1950s. The description is remarkably similar to what people still report today.

Motorists were already visiting the hill, leaving confused and trying to work out what had happened. Gilbert wrote that some people even took their cars to a garage looking for a mechanical or magnetic explanation. The hill was sometimes described as electrified.

The physical road did not need to change for the legend to grow. Once people saw a car move in a direction their eyes labelled uphill, almost any invisible force sounded possible.

Then came the ghost stories

Visser, who has documented the hill for years, told BurlingtonToday that he has heard stories blaming ghosts for the apparent pull. He does not believe them.

There is no evidence of a supernatural force on King Road, and there is no established magnetic anomaly pulling vehicles along it. The phenomenon belongs to a much larger family of places usually called magnetic hills, gravity hills or antigravity hills.

They have been convincing people in different countries for the same reason.

The answer is not in the car

In 2003, researchers from the University of Padova tested antigravity hills in a series of laboratory experiments. They changed the slope of model roads, the surrounding inclines and the position of the visible horizon.

The result was consistent: a slight downhill grade can look uphill when the surrounding landscape gives the eye the wrong reference point. The researchers traced the effect to a misreading of eye level relative to gravity, especially when contextual slopes or a false horizon distort what looks level.

King Road has the right ingredients.

Visser's local explanation is that the apparent uphill section begins near the top of a smaller rise while a larger hill stands farther down the road. Looking toward that larger hill tilts the scene upward. Between the two, the pavement actually bows slightly downward, even though it does not look that way from the usual viewpoint.

Burlington News has not independently surveyed the road's elevation profile, so that local description is not being presented as our own measurement. But it matches the mechanism researchers have demonstrated at antigravity hills elsewhere: gravity still pulls downhill while the landscape convinces the eye that downhill is up.

Why it still feels wrong after you know the answer

The explanation is almost less satisfying than the mystery because nothing unusual has to happen to the vehicle. The engine is not fighting gravity. A hidden magnet is not pulling the chassis. The road does not reverse its slope.

Your visual system is making a very confident mistake.

Trees, rising terrain and the absence of a clear horizon provide enough context for the brain to build the wrong version of level. Once that happens, ordinary downhill motion looks impossible.

That is also why the King Road story has lasted. The Burlington Historical Society was writing about confused motorists in the 1950s. More than 70 years later, people are still stopping to look.

King Road is narrow and has little room at the roadside. BurlingtonToday also noted the safety risk around people trying to recreate the effect in traffic. This article does not provide instructions for testing it with a vehicle.

We included King Road in our collection of Burlington mysteries, but it is the rare one where the evidence points to an answer. The road itself is ordinary. The unsettling part is how easily the landscape can make ordinary gravity look impossible.
