Can a teacher take your phone? Seven Ontario school rules students should know.

A Burlington guide to phones, detention, backpack searches, school investigations and the posts you make after class.

By Burlington NewsAugust 24, 20266-minute read

School rules are not the same as the rules on a public sidewalk. Principals have legal duties to keep order and students have privacy rights, but the balance changes inside a school. Here is what the current Ontario policy and Halton District School Board procedure actually say.

Usually, yes

Can a teacher take your phone?

Ontario requires students in Grades 7 to 12 to keep phones out of view and stored during instructional time unless an educator permits the device for learning, a medical need or a special-education need. Kindergarten to Grade 6 students are expected to keep devices stored for the full school day.

If a student does not follow the direction, the school can use progressive discipline. That can include having the device surrendered. The provincial rule does not give a school permanent ownership of the phone, and each school should explain how and when a confiscated device is returned.

Not automatically

Can school staff read everything on your phone?

Taking a phone out of a student's hand is not the same as receiving unlimited permission to search its contents. The Halton procedure permits searches when there are reasonable grounds to believe evidence of a serious code-of-conduct breach will be found, but it does not give staff blanket access to every message, photo and account.

Ask what rule is being investigated and why the search is considered necessary. Do not physically interfere. If police are involved, the legal rules are different and students should ask to contact a parent, guardian or lawyer.

Only with a reason

Can a principal search your backpack or jacket?

Yes, when the principal or designate has reasonable and probable grounds to believe there was a serious breach and that the search will reveal evidence. HDSB lists weapons, illegal substances and stolen goods as examples. When possible, the search should happen in the student's presence.

Random or arbitrary searches are not permitted. A rumour is not automatically enough, but credible information from a student, several students, staff observations or a combination can supply reasonable grounds.

Yes, with reasonable cause

Can the school open your locker?

HDSB treats lockers and desks as board property. A principal or designate may search them without advance notice when there is reasonable cause to believe the search will reveal evidence of a rule or discipline breach.

Yes

Can a school give you detention?

Detention is explicitly listed as one possible progressive-discipline consequence in Halton. It should not be automatic or one-size-fits-all. Administrators must consider factors such as age, disability, special-education needs and other circumstances before choosing a consequence.

You may refuse

Do you have to answer every question in a school investigation?

HDSB says school investigations must be fair, objective and procedurally fair. The principal should try to interview everyone involved and keep notes. The procedure also says a student's refusal to participate cannot be held against them.

That does not stop a school from reaching a decision using other credible evidence. A school uses the balance-of-probabilities standard, meaning it decides what was more likely than not to have happened.

Sometimes

Can you be disciplined for a post made away from school?

Yes, if the conduct affects the school climate. HDSB's code applies on school property, buses and school activities, but it can also apply to off-campus behaviour connected to school, including cyberbullying or threats that make students feel unsafe.

If you think a search is unjustified: stay calm. Ask which rule staff believe was broken, what they are looking for and why they believe a search is necessary. You can say, “I do not consent to this search.” Do not physically interfere. Ask for the principal and request that a parent or guardian be contacted. Write down who was present and what happened as soon as you can. If police are involved, ask to speak with a parent, guardian or lawyer.
Not legal advice: This guide gives general Ontario information. A school’s code can add procedures, but it cannot override Ontario law or human-rights obligations. Individual facts matter.
